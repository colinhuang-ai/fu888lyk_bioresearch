## Abstract

### Background

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) remains one of the most prevalent urological malignancies. Although targeted therapies have expanded therapeutic options, a substantial proportion of patients still experience poor outcomes, underscoring the need for more effective and less toxic strategies. Ticagrelor, a novel antiplatelet agent, has recently shown antineoplastic potential in several cancers; however, its role and mechanism in RCC remain undefined.

### Methods

#### In vitro

effects of ticagrelor on RCC cells were evaluated using CCK-8, EdU, and AO/PI assays for proliferation/apoptosis, wound healing and Transwell assays for migration/invasion. *In vivo* efficacy was assessed in an RCC cell-derived xenograft (CDX) model. Network pharmacology and Western blotting were employed to elucidate the underlying mechanism.

### Results

Ticagrelor suppressed proliferation, migration, invasion and adhesion, and promoted apoptosis in RCC cells. In CDX mice, tumor growth was significantly inhibited. Mechanistically, the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway was identified as the key mediator of ticagrelor’s antitumor activity in RCC.

### Conclusions

Ticagrelor exerts anti-RCC effects *via* blockade of the EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling axis, highlighting its translational potential and positioning this pathway as a therapeutic target and possible biomarker in RCC.

**Keywords:** Renal cancer, ticagrelor, EGFR, PI3K, AKT

## Introduction

Renal carcinoma is one of the most common malignancies in the urinary system, originating from the kidneys [[1]]. According to global cancer statistics, the annual incidence of renal carcinoma is approximately 400,000 cases, with annual mortality figures nearing 175,000 [[2]]. It ranks as the tenth most common cancer worldwide [[2]]. From an epidemiological perspective, it is important to consider that novel favorable subsets of cancers of unknown primary (CUP) have been delineated, most notably an RCC-like CUP subtype. This distinct clinical entity, managed according to RCC treatment protocols, likely contributes in part to the rising incidence trends observed in RCC [[3]]. The clinical manifestations of renal carcinoma are diverse, with early symptoms often being non-specific. Diagnosis frequently occurs at advanced stages, resulting in a poor prognosis for patients [[4]]. However, continuous advancements in diagnostic technologies are improving early detection rates. Notably, exosomes – nanosized extracellular vesicles with a protective bilayer membrane – have emerged as a promising source of non-invasive biomarkers [[5]]. Their cargo, including miRNAs and proteins, is highly stable and can be detected in patient serum and urine, offering sensitive tools for early RCC diagnosis and monitoring [[6]]. The main types of renal carcinoma include RCC, renal pelvic carcinoma, and carcinoma of the renal tubules, with RCC constituting approximately 80% of all renal cancer cases [[7]]. Its pathogenesis is complex, involving multiple factors such as genetics, environment, and lifestyle [[8]]. The main treatment for renal cancer is surgical intervention, especially in the early stages [[9]]. However, for patients with locally advanced or metastatic renal cancer, surgery alone often fails to achieve the desired therapeutic effect [[10]]. Therefore, other treatment methods need to be combined to improve survival rates and quality of life. In recent years, systemic therapies for advanced RCC have expanded to include targeted agents and immunotherapies. Targeted therapies, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) against vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) and inhibitors of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, have shown efficacy in improving progression-free survival [[11]]. Additionally, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting programmed death-1 (PD-1) or its ligand (PD-L1) have revolutionized treatment by providing durable responses in some patients [[12]]. The development of such strategies is increasingly guided by molecular pathogenesis. In clear-cell RCC (ccRCC), the most common subtype, loss of the VHL protein leads to constitutive stabilization and activity of HIF-2α, a key oncogenic driver [[13]]. Targeting this axis with the HIF-2α inhibitor belzutifan has demonstrated significant clinical benefit in advanced, pretreated ccRCC, underscoring the therapeutic potential of precision oncology in this disease [[14]]. Despite these advances, limitations persist, including primary or acquired resistance, significant toxicities, and variable response rates, underscoring the need for novel therapeutic strategies [[15]].

Ticagrelor is a novel P2Y12 receptor antagonist that is widely used in the treatment of acute coronary syndrome. In recent years, studies have found potential applications of ticagrelor in cancer treatment, particularly showing positive effects in inhibiting tumor cell proliferation and metastasis [[16,17]]. Research shows that ticagrelor significantly reduces the growth and metastasis of lung adenocarcinoma cells, and its mechanism is related to the downregulation of the SYK (spleen tyrosine kinase) signaling pathway and the PI3K/Akt pathway [[18]]. In studies of glioma, ticagrelor has been found to exert anti-tumor effects by inhibiting the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process [[19]]. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), ticagrelor inhibits the migration and invasion of TNBC cells by suppressing PI3K [[20]]. However, the effects and mechanisms of ticagrelor on renal cancer remain unclear.

Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is an important tyrosine kinase involved in various biological processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival [[21]]. EGFR promotes cell proliferation and survival by activating downstream signaling pathways, such as the Ras-Raf-MAPK and PI3K-AKT pathways, through binding with its ligands [[22]]. Studies have shown that EGFR is a driving factor in tumorigenesis, particularly in lung cancer, breast cancer, and glioblastoma [[23]]. EGFR signaling pathway serves as a therapeutic target for human cancers [[24]]. EGFR inhibitors, such as erlotinib and gefitinib, have been used in the clinical treatment of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). In renal cancer, the overexpression of EGFR is closely related to tumor occurrence and progression. Research indicates that the activation of EGFR can enhance the migration and invasion capabilities of renal cancer cells, providing potential targets for the treatment of renal cancer [[25,26]].

Ticagrelor, as a new type of antiplatelet drug, has not yet fully explored its potential in tumor treatment. This study aims to investigate the inhibitory effect of ticagrelor on RCC and its potential mechanisms, thereby providing new ideas for clinical treatment. In addition, studying the mechanism of action of ticagrelor, especially its impact on the EGFR/IP3K/AKT signaling pathway, will provide new perspectives for understanding the occurrence and development of renal cancer.

## Materials and methods

### Cells and animals

786-O, Caki-1, ACHN and OS-RC-2 were purchased from Baidi Biotech Ltd. and authenticated *via* STR profiling. Female Balb/c nude mice (8–12 weeks old) were purchased from Vital River Laboratories. All the mouse experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanchang Royo Biotech Co,. Ltd (Approval No. RYE2023030601). All mice were anesthetized using isoflurane (4% for induction, 1.5–2% for maintenance). Euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation at the experimental endpoint, in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 edition). The study had adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines.

### Cell proliferation assay by CCK-8

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (C0039, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well. After allowing cells to adhere, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing ticagrelor in various concentrations of 0 μM, 2.5 μM, 5 μM, 10 μM, 20 μM, 40 μM, 60 μM, 80 μM, 100 μM. Following 48 h of treatment, 10 µL of the CCK-8 reagent was added directly to each well. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO<sub>2</sub> for 4 h. The absorbance of each well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek). The optical density (OD) value at 450 nm is directly proportional to the number of viable cells.

### EdU fluorescence labeling for cell proliferation

Added EdU solution (C0075S, Beyotime, China) to cell culture medium at 10 μM concentration, incubate cells for 2 h. Then, removed the medium, washed cells with PBS, and fixed them using 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After washing again with PBS, permeabilized cells with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. Prepared the Click reaction solution according to the requirements of the test kit, and incubated cells with the mixture in the dark for 30 min. Finally, washed cells thoroughly with PBS to remove unreacted reagents, and visualize and analyze fluorescently labeled proliferating cells by fluorescence microscopy.

### AO/PI staining

Prepared a mixture of acridine orange (AO) and propidium iodide (PI) staining solution (C2017S, Beyotime, China). Removed the culture medium from cells, washed them gently with PBS, then added the prepared AO/PI staining solution and incubated at room temperature for 10 min in the dark. After incubation, removed the staining solution and wash the cells with PBS again. Finally, visualize the cells immediately under a fluorescence microscope, where live cells will exhibit green fluorescence, while dead cells will show red fluorescence.

### Wound healing assay

Seeded cells into 6-well plates at an appropriate density and cultured until they reach approximately 90% confluence; used a sterile 200 μL pipette tip to create straight scratches across the cell monolayer, then gently washed the wells twice with PBS to remove detached cells; added fresh serum-free medium including 0 μM, 10 μM or 20 μM ticagrelor, and incubated the plates at 37 °C with 5% CO<sub>2</sub>, captured images of the scratch area at 0, 4 and 8 h using an inverted microscope with a fixed magnification, and analyze cell migration by measuring the remaining scratch width or the migrated cell area with image analysis software.

### Transwell migration assay

Pre-coated the polycarbonate membrane of the Transwell insert with Matrigel for invasion assays, then equilibrated the insert in serum – free medium at 37 °C with 5% CO<sub>2</sub> for 30 min; trypsinized and resuspended cells in serum – free medium, and seeded an appropriate number of cells into the upper chamber of the Transwell insert, while adding medium containing 10% FBS to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant; incubated the Transwell plate at 37 °C with 5% CO<sub>2</sub> for 24 h; after incubation, removed non-migrated or non-invaded cells from the upper surface of the membrane with a cotton swab, fixed and stained the cells on the lower surface of the membrane with 4% paraformaldehyde and 0.1% crystal violet, respectively; finally, washed the insert with PBS, air-dried it, and counted the stained cells under a microscope in multiple randomly selected fields of view to assess cell migration or invasion ability.

### Cell adhesion assay

Pre-coated 96-well plates with an appropriate Matrigel and incubate overnight at 4 °C. Discarded the coating solution, washed the wells with PBS, and blocked them with 1% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Trypsinized and resuspended cells in serum-free medium, then seeded a specific number of cells into the pre-treated wells and incubate at 37 °C with 5% CO<sub>2</sub> for the desired time period (0, 10, 20 min), gently washed the wells with PBS to remove non-adherent cells, fix the adherent cells with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Take pictures with a microscope and count the number of adherent cells.

### *In vivo* efficacy evaluation of the renal cancer CDX model

Establish subcutaneous renal cancer CDX models by injecting 5 × 10<sup>6</sup> 786-O renal cancer cells into the left flank of Balb/c nude mice, when the tumor volume reaches approximately 1000 mm³, removed the tumor and inoculated it under the skin of the next batch of Balb/c nude mice. When the tumor volume reaches approximately 50–100 mm³, randomly divided the mice into two groups (*n* = 6 per group, sample size was determined based on preliminary data and common practice in similar xenograft studies to achieve sufficient statistical power [[18]]): vehicle control group (administered with the solvent) and ticagrelor group (P.O., q.d., 100 mg/kg). And measured the body weight and tumor volume (calculated as length × width<sup>2</sup>/2) of the mice every 3 or 4 days. At the end of the experiment, sacrificed the mice, excised the tumors, weighed them and took pictures. Group allocation was blinded the investigators during drug administration and outcome assessment (tumor measurement, endpoint analysis).

### Network pharmacology analysis

The chemical structure of ticagrelor was retrieved from PubChem database. Potential targets of ticagrelor were predicted using SwissTargetPrediction and PharmMapper platforms. Kidney cancer-related genes were obtained from GeneCards, DisGeNET, and OMIM databases. The predicted drug targets and disease-associated genes were intersected to identify the key targets of ticagrelor in kidney cancer. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of the key targets were performed using DAVID database. A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of the key targets was constructed *via* STRING database and visualized with Cytoscape software. The topological properties of the PPI network were analyzed, and hub genes were screened out. For KEGG pathway analysis, DAVID was used to perform enrichment analysis on these targets, revealing significant pathways (*p* < 0.05). GO enrichment analysis was also conducted *via* DAVID, covering biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. Finally, molecular docking was carried out using AutoDock Vina. The 3D structure of ticagrelor was obtained from PubChem, and protein structures of hub genes were retrieved from the RCSB PDB. After preparation (removal of water molecules, addition of polar hydrogens), docking simulations were performed, and binding energies and interaction modes were analyzed.

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using Gromacs 2022. The AMBER14SB and GAFF2 force fields were applied to the receptor and ligand, respectively. The system was solvated in a TIP3P water box with 1 nm padding and neutralized with ions. Long-range electrostatics were treated with Particle Mesh Ewald (1 nm cutoff). Bonds were constrained with SHAKE, and integration utilized a 1 fs time step *via* the Verlet leapfrog algorithm. Energy minimization was conducted with 3000 steps of steepest descent followed by 2000 steps of conjugate gradient, gradually releasing positional restraints. A 100 ns NPT simulation was performed at 310 K, with subsequent analysis of Root-Mean-Square Deviation (RMSD), Root-Mean-Square Fluctuation (RMSF), hydrogen bonds, Radius of Gyration (Rg), and SASA using standard Gromacs tools.

### Immunoblotting

The cells were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated *via* SDS – PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The proteins were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against EGFR (66455-1-Ig, Proteintech, China), p-EGFR (Tyr1068), PI3K (YM6978, Immunoway, China), p-PI3K (YT3709, Immunoway, China), AKT(YM3618, Immunoway, China), p-AKT (YP0590, Immunoway, China), and GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech, China). After three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies including anti-mouse (RS0092, Immunoway, China) and anti-rabbit (RS0002, Immunoway, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, with GAPDH serving as the internal control.

### Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0; GraphPad Software, USA). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC<sub>50</sub>) values for cell proliferation were calculated using non-linear regression (log(inhibitor) vs. response -Variable slope model). For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was used. For the *in vivo* tumor volume data over time, a two-way ANOVA with repeated measures followed by Sidak’s post-hoc test was employed. A p -value of less than 0.05 (*p* < 0.05) was considered statistically significant, with **p* < 0.05, ***p* < 0.01, and p* < 0.001 used to denote different levels of significance throughout the figures.

## Result

### Ticagrelor inhibited the proliferation of renal cancer cell lines and promoted their apoptosis

The inhibitory effects of ticagrelor were assessed in renal cancer cell lines (786-O, Caki-1, ACHN, and OS-RC-2) using the CCK-8 cell proliferation assay. Results demonstrated that ticagrelor inhibited the proliferation of 786-O, Caki-1, ACHN, and OS-RC-2 cells in a dose-dependent manner after 48 h of treatment (Figure 1A). The IC50 values of ticagrelor were determined as follows: 16.73 μM for 786-O cells, 33.96 μM for Caki-1 cells, 29.19 μM for ACHN cells, and 65.56 μM for OS-RC-2 cells (Figure 1A). Additionally, EdU staining was employed to evaluate the impact of ticagrelor treated for 24 h on 786-O cell and ACHN proliferation. Ticagrelor significantly suppressed 786-O cell proliferation at a concentration of 5 μM and 10 μM, and also inhibited ACHN cell proliferation at a concentration of 10 μM and 20 μM (Figure 1B-E). Finally, acridine orange/propidium iodide (AO/PI) staining was utilized to examine ticagrelor-induced apoptosis in 786-O and ACHN cell lines. Ticagrelor treatment significantly promoted apoptosis in 786-O cells and ACHN for 48 h (Figure 1F-I).

### Ticagrelor inhibited the invasion, migration and adhesion of renal cancer cell lines

The processes of tumor development are directly influenced by the characteristics of tumor invasion, migration, and adhesion. The impact of ticagrelor on the invasive capacity of 786-O cells was evaluated using a wound healing assay. Results demonstrated that ticagrelor significantly reduced the migration capacity of 786-O and ACHN cells at concentrations of 10 and 20 μM after 24 and 48 h, respectively, compared with the control group (Figure 2A-C). Subsequently, a Transwell migration assay was employed to assess the effect of ticagrelor on 786-O cell migration. Ticagrelor (10 μM or 20 μM) significantly inhibited the migration of 786-O and ACHN cells (Figure 2D-F). Finally, a cell adhesion assay was conducted to examine the influence of ticagrelor on 786-O and ACHN cell adhesion after 10 min and 15 min of cell quiescence. After 48 h of ticagrelor (15 μM or 20 μM) treatment, it significantly inhibited the adhesion of the 786-O and ACHN cell lines (Figure 2G-J).

### Ticagrelor inhibited the growth of renal cancer cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) model *in vivo*

To further validate the *in vivo* effects of ticagrelor on renal cancer, a cell-derived xenograft (CDX) model using the 786-O cell line was established to assess the impact of ticagrelor. Ticagrelor treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth volume in the 786-O xenografts at day 21 and 24 of administration, compared to the control group. Furthermore, tumor weight was significantly reduced following 24 days of ticagrelor treatment. Importantly, ticagrelor administration did not significantly affect the body weight of the mice (Figure 3A-D). The results demonstrate that ticagrelor significantly suppresses the growth of 786-O cell-derived xenograft (CDX) tumors *in vivo*.

### Using network pharmacology to identify the key targets and pathways of ticagrelor in renal cancer

The SMILES code of ticagrelor was retrieved from the PubChem database and input into the Swiss Target Prediction database for target prediction. Results indicated that ticagrelor possesses 100 potential targets. Subsequently, disease-associated targets for Renal cancer were identified by searching the GeneCards database. Targets with a Relevance Score ≥ 10 were selected, yielding 2,109 renal cancer-associated targets. The predicted targets of ticagrelor and the final screened renal cancer-associated targets were imported into Venny 2.1 to identify overlapping targets. This analysis revealed 44 common targets (MET, EGFR, HRAS, REN, FGFR2, FGFR1, TNF, SLC5A2, NTRK1, KDR, MAPK1, MDM2, PPARG, MAP2K1, MMP9, AURKA, MMP2, PTPN11, IGF1R, AXL, JAK2, CHEK1, PRKDC, CDK2, LGALS3, GAPDH, MAPK14, PIK3CD, SGK1, F2, HSP90AA1, PIK3CB, GCK, KLK3, BTK, CDK1, EPHB4, MMP13, IRAK4, CXCR2, SLC5A1, PDPK1, LGALS9, MDM4) (Figure 4A). A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed for these common targets using the STRING database, facilitating systematic visualization of target interrelationships. Core hub targets identified through topological analysis included EGFR, HSP90AA1, JAK2, PIK3CD, HRAS, PIK3CB, PTPN11, TNF, and NET (Figure 4B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis demonstrated significant associations with EGFR signaling pathway (*Biological processes*), ATP binding (*Molecular functions*) and so on (Figure 4C). KEGG pathway analysis revealed enrichment in pathways in cancer, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, Ras signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway and so on (Figure 4D). Collectively, these results suggest that EGFR and its downstream PI3K-AKT pathway may constitute critical therapeutic targets for ticagrelor-mediated suppression of renal carcinoma.

(B) PPI network was constructed for common targets of ticagrelor and renal cancer. (C) GO Analysis. (D) KEGG Analysis. (E) Molecular docking of ticagrelor with EGFR, PI3KCB, PI3KCD and AKT. (F) Molecular Dynamics Simulation of Ticagrelor and AKT, the time-dependent RMSD values, Rg values, SASA values, HBonds values and RMSF values of AKT- ticagrelor.

Molecular docking was performed to investigate the interactions between ticagrelor and EGFR, PI3KCB, PI3KCD, and AKT. The results revealed that ticagrelor exhibited relatively low binding affinities with EGFR, PI3KCB, and PI3KCD, with binding energies of −4.44 kcal/mol, −5.23 kcal/mol, and −5.21 kcal/mol, respectively (Figure 4E). In contrast, a significantly stronger binding energy of −8.01 kcal/mol was observed with AKT (Figure 4E), indicating a higher binding affinity between ticagrelor and AKT. Consequently, AKT was selected for further molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with ticagrelor (Figure 4F). Root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis showed that the complex system reached equilibrium after 5 ns, with the RMSD value stabilizing around 1.4 Å, suggesting conformational stability upon binding (Figure 4F). The radius of gyration (Rg) fluctuated only slightly, indicating a compact structure without significant expansion or contraction (Figure 4F). Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) analysis revealed no notable changes before and after binding, further supporting the stability of the complex (Figure 4F). Hydrogen bond analysis demonstrated that an average of approximately 4 hydrogen bonds were formed between the ligand and the protein, with a maximum of up to 8 bonds, reflecting strong hydrogen bonding interactions (Figure 4F). Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) results indicated that most amino acid residues exhibited fluctuations below 2 Å, suggesting low overall flexibility and high structural stability of the complex (Figure 4F). In summary, these results demonstrate that ticagrelor forms a stable complex with the target protein AKT, with favorable molecular interactions.

### Ticagrelor suppressed RCC growth *via* the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway

To investigate the mechanism of action of ticagrelor, the RCC cell line 786-O was treated with the EGFR inhibitor gefitinib. Experimental results showed that gefitinib alone exhibited limited inhibitory efficacy against 786-O cells (inhibition rate < 30% at 0.1-10 μM) (Figure 5A). In contrast, the combination of ticagrelor (5 μM or 10 μM) and gefitinib (5 μM or 10 μM) significantly enhanced the inhibition rate of 786-O cells than alone ticagrelor or gefitinib treated (Figure 5B). Using Western blot analysis, we assessed the protein expression levels of p-EGFR, EGFR, p-PI3K, PI3K, p-AKT, and AKT in 786-O cells treated with ticagrelor (10 μM), gefitinib (10 μM), or their combination. The results demonstrated that ticagrelor reduced the expression of p-EGFR, p-PI3K, and p-AKT proteins (Figure 5C-I). Furthermore, the combination of ticagrelor and gefitinib led to a more pronounced suppression of p-EGFR, p-PI3K, and p-AKT expression (Figure 5C-I). These findings suggest that ticagrelor inhibits renal cancer growth potentially through the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway, and gefitinib can enhance the inhibitory effect of ticagrelor.

## Discussion

The results of this study have confirmed that ticagrelor has a significant inhibitory effect on renal cancer. Ticagrelor, as a commercially available drug, has already demonstrated its safety. Compared to general chemotherapy drugs, it has fewer adverse reactions. These facts provide a solid theoretical foundation for the subsequent clinical application of ticagrelor in the treatment of renal cancer. Ticagrelor, as a classic antiplatelet aggregation drug, will have a more significant effect in tumor patients with thrombosis.

The study revealed that EGFR was a key target for ticagrelor in the treatment of renal cancer. The findings of this study provide critical insights into the molecular mechanisms through which ticagrelor exerts its effects on renal cancer cells. It was reported that the overexpression rate of EGFR in renal cancer tissues was as high as 40-80% [[27]], and its high expression was associated with higher tumor grade, invasive, metastatic disease, poorer survival rate, and resistance to treatment [[27–29]]. It was reported that EGFR is a therapeutic target for patient-derived renal cancer cells [[30]]. EGFR inhibitors such as erlotinib and gefitinib have been used as first-line treatment drugs for non-small cell lung cancer [[31]]. Furthermore, EGFR inhibitors had demonstrated efficacy in other cancers [[32]]. The results showed that the combination of ticagrelor and the EGFR inhibitor gefitinib suppressed the proliferation of RCC cells, suggesting that ticagrelor held promise as a potential therapeutic agent, either alone or in combination, targeting EGFR for RCC treatment.

This study demonstrated that ticagrelor blocked the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway in RCC. Previous literature reports indicated that PI3K/AKT, as a major downstream signaling pathway of EGFR, plays crucial roles in tumor cell proliferation and apoptosis; specifically, CEP55 promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), proliferation, and metastasis in RCC through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [[33]], while acylglycerol kinase inhibits invasion and metastasis of RCC cells *via* PI3K/AKT signaling [[34]]. Furthermore, down-regulation of lactotransferrin, a key component of the innate immune system, has been shown to promote metastasis in RCC [[35,36]]. Intriguingly, this same down-regulation enhances tumor cell responsiveness to mTOR inhibitors, highlighting its potential as a predictive biomarker for therapy [[36]]. Thus, ticagrelor likely exerts its effects in RCC by suppressing the PI3K/AKT pathway through EGFR inhibition.

Ticagrelor, the first oral and reversible P2Y12 receptor antagonist globally, bound to a non-ADP-competitive site, inducing a conformational change in the receptor that thereby blocked ADP-triggered GP IIb/IIIa activation and platelet aggregation [[37]]. Recent studies suggested platelets accumulated in primary tumors, promoting tumor growth and angiogenesis through growth factor release [[38]]. *In vitro* studies demonstrated that ticagrelor-mediated P2Y12 receptor blockade impaired epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and platelet-induced migration in HT29 cells [[39]]. In the 4T1 murine breast cancer model, ticagrelor significantly reduced circulating tumor cell (CTC)-platelet aggregates and pulmonary metastases while prolonging survival [[40]]. The network pharmacology results indicated ticagrelor affected pathways in RCC including ATP binding and platelet-derived growth factor receptor-alpha (PDGFRα) signaling. Given renal vascularity, platelets were likely to influence RCC progression. Ticagrelor might also attenuate platelet-tumor crosstalk by antagonizing P2Y12 receptors, reducing ADP-induced platelet release and diminishing paracrine signaling of EGFR ligands (e.g. HB-EGF) mediated by these interactions.

This study offered the advantage of investigating ticagrelor’s effects on RCC through both *in vitro* cancer cells and a CDX model, while simultaneously identifying a key target pathway, thereby proposing a potential therapeutic agent for clinical RCC treatment. However, several limitations were noted: only two human cell lines and one murine xenograft model were utilized, with more clinically relevant models such as organoids or patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) not being employed. The mechanism of EGFR inhibition – whether direct or mediated indirectly through P2Y12-EGFR crosstalk – remained unresolved. Due to the absence of renal cancer incidence and survival data from cardiovascular patients receiving ticagrelor, the compound’s clinical antitumor efficacy could not be ascertained. Furthermore, the lack of clinical RCC tissue samples prevented analysis of the correlation between tumor tissue EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway activity and therapeutic response. Finally, EGFR’s validation as a therapeutic target in RCC required additional clinical experimental evidence.

## Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the antiplatelet drug ticagrelor exerted significant anti-tumor effects against RCC by effectively suppressing cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and adhesion *in vitro*, and potently inhibiting tumor growth *in vivo*. Mechanistic investigations, which integrated network pharmacology, molecular docking, dynamics simulations, and experimental validation, identified the EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway as the primary mediator of ticagrelor’s antitumor efficacy. Furthermore, the enhanced inhibitory effect observed with the combination of ticagrelor and gefitinib highlighted the potential for therapeutic synergy through targeting this pathway. These findings elucidated a novel molecular mechanism of action for ticagrelor and established its repurposing potential as a candidate for RCC treatment, thereby providing a foundation for future therapeutic development.
