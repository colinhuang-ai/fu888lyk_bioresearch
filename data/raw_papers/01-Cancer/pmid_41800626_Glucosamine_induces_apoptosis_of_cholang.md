# Glucosamine induces apoptosis of cholangiocarcinoma cells by suppressing high-mannose type *N*-glycosylation and EGFR/STAT3 signaling

- **Tác giả:** Orawan Waenphimai, Cheerapinya Taebprakhon, Chatchai Phoomak, Chen Chu, Atit Silsirivanit, Sopit Wongkham, Charupong Saengboonmee
- **Tạp chí:** Future science OA (2026)
- **PMID:** [41800626](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41800626/)
- **PMCID:** [PMC12973475](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12973475/)
- **DOI:** [10.1080/20565623.2026.2641244](https://doi.org/10.1080/20565623.2026.2641244)

## Abstract

### Aim

This study aimed to investigate the effects of glucosamine, a glucose analogue mainly transported via glucose transporter (GLUT) 2, on the progression of cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) cells.

### Methods

Differential expressions of GLUTs in CCA and normal bile ducts were analyzed using a public transcriptomic dataset. The effects of glucosamine on CCA cell viability and proliferation were examined using an MTT assay and flow cytometry. Molecular mechanisms were investigated using Western blots and lectin blots.

### Results

GLUT2 expression was significantly decreased in CCA compared with the normal bile duct tissues. Glucosamine significantly reduced CCA cell viability in a dose-dependent manner (*p* < .05), while flow cytometry showed increased G1 cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in glucosamine-treated cells (*p* < .05). Glucosamine significantly suppressed high-mannose type *N*-glycosylation in CCA cells, as shown in concanavalin A lectin blots and the Western blots of glycoprotein 130 (*p* < .05), leading to a reduced epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) expression and STAT3 phosphorylation. Cell viability and high-mannose type *N*-glycosylation could be partially rescued by high-glucose supplementation, confirming that disrupting high-mannose type *N*-glycosylation and EGFR/STAT3 signaling are part of the underlying mechanisms.

### Conclusion

Glucosamine exerted anti-cancer effects on CCA cells, suggesting its potential for further study as a repurposing drug for CCA.

**Keywords:** Cholangiocarcinoma, food supplement, glucosamine, intracellular signaling, *N*-glycosylation

## Introduction

Deregulated cellular metabolism is one of the malignant hallmarks of cancer [[1,2]]. It is a key characteristic that has been studied for more than a decade since the Warburg effect was discovered [[3]]. The reprogramming of energy metabolism is advantageous for cancer cells to survive, rapidly grow, and compete with surrounding tissues. Cancer cells upregulate numerous genes that benefit them in energy metabolism, including those involved in glucose uptake and glycolysis [[4]]. An exceptionally high glucose requirement for energy metabolism then becomes one of the vulnerabilities that could be targeted for cancer treatment [[5]]. In addition, many reports indicate that high glucose requirements in cancer cells may not only benefit the energy supply but also be necessary for the high levels of glycosylation [[6,7]]. Since aberrant glycosylation is one of the factors that promote cancer progression, it usually leads to a poor prognosis for patients. A comprehensive investigation of cancer metabolism in both energy and associated pathways would then be crucial for the development of effective cancer therapies.

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is a malignancy arising in the bile duct epithelia. It is the second most prevalent primary liver cancer, accounting for 15–30% of all liver cancer cases, and approximately 3% of all gastrointestinal cancers [[8]]. The highest CCA incidences are reported in East and Southeast Asia, particularly Thailand, South Korea, and China [[9]]. The etiology of CCA in this region is strongly associated with liver fluke infection, which results in a unique biological background and characteristics [[10,11]]. Previous studies demonstrated that metabolic reprogramming is one of the factors promoting CCA aggressiveness, especially increased glucose uptake and abnormal glycosylation [[12]]. The gradually upregulated glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) [[13]] and hexokinase II (HK2) [[14]] have been reported in the liver fluke-associated carcinogenesis of CCA in a hamster model. High GLUT1 expression in CCA tissues is also associated with shorter patient survival outcomes [[13,15]]. Moreover, CCA cells cultured in high-glucose media or inoculated into animals with underlying diabetes exhibited a more rapid growth rate than those in euglycemic conditions [[16]]. The high glucose requirement of CCA cells was found to be a key factor that activates the intracellular signaling pathways and leads to dysregulated glycosylation, resulting in more aggressive phenotypes [[17,18]]. Understanding the network of carbohydrate metabolism and glycosylation in CCA might be a further strategy to overcome the current limitations of CCA treatment.

Although metabolic reprogramming can benefit cancer cells by promoting growth and invasiveness, it can also lead to metabolic vulnerability if cancer cells are highly addicted to specific nutrients or metabolites. Upregulation of proteins and enzymes involved in energy metabolism or their associated pathways can serve as therapeutic targets for several cancers, including CCA [[12]]. For example, the high expression of GLUTs facilitates glucose utilization in CCA cells, which could be targeted with a specific inhibitor or used as a route for the transport of glucose analogues to inhibit glycolysis within cells [[13]]. Using glucose analogues such as 2-deoxy-D-glucose or glucosamine has, thereby, a high potential to disrupt glucose metabolism in cancer cells. This could yield anti-tumor effects that may pave the way for targeted therapy in the future.

Glucosamine (2-amino-2-deoxy-glucose) is an amino sugar with a structure almost similar to glucose, except that the hydroxyl group at C-2 is substituted by an amino (–NH<sub>2</sub>) group. Glucosamine is a precursor for several polysaccharides, glycosylated peptides, and glycoproteins such as proteoglycans, glycosaminoglycans, and hyaluronic acids [[19]]. Studies have shown that glucosamine may delay cartilage destruction and promote the synthesis of chondroitin sulfate in joints and soft tissues. Thus, it has been used as a food supplement or alternative treatment for osteoarthritis in some regions [[20]]. Studies in patients who used regular glucosamine as food supplements found that use of glucosamine is associated with a reduced risk of developing particular cancers, such as lung and colorectal cancers [[21–23]]. Preclinical experiments have also shown that glucosamine can suppress the growth and induce apoptosis in several cancer cell types, including hepatocellular carcinoma [[24,25]], through various mechanisms. One proposed mechanism is that the high similarity of glucosamine and glucose structures results in common cellular uptake and metabolic pathways in mammalian cells [[24]]. Glucosamine can be transported through GLUT1 and GLUT2, and it can also be phosphorylated by HK. Thus, it could compete with glucose in glycolysis and associated pathways [[24,25]], such as the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway. This could disrupt cellular energy metabolism and glycosylation pathways in cancer cells, resulting in the suppression of cancer progression. Although the effects of glucose metabolism and glycosylation have been well-studied in CCA, the understanding of how glucosamine affects CCA cell biology remains unclear. This study, therefore, aimed to investigate the therapeutic effects of glucosamine on CCA cells and their associated molecular mechanisms. Given that glucosamine has been used as a food supplement and alternative medicine in clinical practice in some regions, understanding its effects in CCA might provide the opportunity to repurpose glucosamine as a therapeutic agent for CCA in the near future.

## Materials and methods

### Cell lines and reagents

CCA cell lines, KKU-055 and KKU-213A, were established from liver fluke-associated CCA as previously described [[26]] and obtained from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB) Cell Bank, Osaka, Japan. The immortalized cholangiocyte cell line (MMNK-1) was a gift from Dr. N. Kobayashi [[27]]. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Grand Island, NY) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO<sub>2</sub>. D-glucosamine hydrochloride (purity ≥ 99%, Sigma-Aldrich) was reconstituted in sterile distilled water and diluted to the desired concentration before use. All experiments using cell lines were repeated with at least three biological replicates. The study protocol has been approved for exemption from ethical clearance by the Khon Kaen University Ethics Committee for Human Research ([HE681134](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/HE681134), Approval date: 7 March 2025), based on the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the ICH-Good Clinical Practice Guideline.

### Transcriptomics data analyses

The GEO datasets were retrieved using the GEO2R tool [[28]]. The [GSE76297](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE76297) dataset of the tumor from Thai CCA cases (*N* = 91) and non-tumor tissues (*N* = 92) was used to analyze differential gene expression [[29]]. Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA).

### Cell viability assay

CCA cells were seeded into 96-well plates (2000 cells/well) overnight and treated with various concentrations of glucosamine (1.5, 3, and 6 mM). A 1% distilled water solution in complete media was used as a vehicle control. Cell viability was measured at 72 h using MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazolyl-2)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) according to the manufacturer’s recommendation (Sigma-Aldrich). Briefly, the MTT reagent was added to the final concentration of 1 mg/mL and incubated in a 37 °C, humidified incubator, for 4 h. Dimethyl sulfoxide was used to dissolve the insoluble formazan complex, and the absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

### Cell cycle analysis

CCA cells were seeded into six-well plates (1 × 10<sup>5</sup> cells/well) and treated with glucosamine (1.5 and 3 mM) for 72 h. Cells were collected by trypsinization and fixed in 70% cold ethanol and stained with propidium iodide (PI) (Invitrogen, Eugene, OR) following the manufacturer’s recommendation. The DNA content was analyzed using the FACSCanto II flow cytometer and FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ).

### Annexin V binding assay

CCA cells were seeded into six-well plates (1 × 10<sup>5</sup> cells) and treated with glucosamine at the indicated concentrations for 72 h. Cells were harvested and incubated with FITC-Annexin V (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Before flow cytometric analysis, propidium iodide was added at a final concentration of 1 µg/mL. Annexin V and PI-stained cells were analyzed using a FACSCanto II flow cytometer and FlowJo software (BD Bioscience).

### SDS-PAGE and Western blots

A total of 20–30 µg protein was separated on 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Immobilon-P; Millipore, MA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk (w/v) in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST), and then incubated with primary antibodies included anti-X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein (XIAP) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX), anti-cyclin D1 (Cell Signaling, Cambridge, MA), anti-p21 (Cell Signaling), anti-caspase 9 (Cell Signaling), anti-caspase 3 (Cell Signaling), anti-cleaved caspase 3 (Cell Signaling), anti-Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) (Cell signaling), anti-cleaved PARP (cell signaling), anti-cyclin dependent kinase (CDK) 4 (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL), anti-CDK6 (Proteintech), anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) (Cell Signaling), anti-signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) (Cell Signaling), anti-pSTAT3 (Y075) (Cell Signaling), and anti-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (EMD Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were incubated with each primary antibody for 1 h at room temperature and kept at 4 °C overnight. The membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated secondary antibody, either anti-rabbit (Cell Signaling) or anti-mouse (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) for 1 h at room temperature. The chemiluminescent signals were detected using a gel documentation system (Amersham<sup>TM</sup> Imager 600, BD Biosciences, NJ, USA), and the band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD).

### Lectin blots

Five micrograms of protein from total cell lysates were separated on a 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membranes were blocked for nonspecific binding with 5% bovine serum albumin (w/v) in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated with 0.25 µg/mL biotinylated lectins as follows; concanavalin A (Con A) (Vector Laboratories, Newark, NJ), wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) (Vector Laboratories), and *Phaseolus Vulgaris* erythroagglutinin (PHA-E) (Vector Laboratories), for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were washed with TBST for 10 min, three times, and incubated with streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase conjugate (Invitrogen, Frederick, MD) for 1 h at room temperature. Then, membranes were washed with TBST for 10 min, three times, and the chemiluminescent signal was detected using a gel documentation system (Amersham<sup>TM</sup> Imager 600). Total band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software.

### Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (*SD*) from three independent experiments. The statistical significance of differences between the control and experimental groups was determined using the Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA when appropriate by IBM SPSS ver 26.0 (Armonk, NY). Statistical significance was set as *p* < .05.

## Results

### GLUT2 was significantly downregulated in CCA tissues

To clarify alterations in glucose and related sugar uptake systems in CCA cells, public transcriptomic data from Thai liver fluke-associated CCA were used to analyze differential expression of common GLUTs (GLUT1-GLUT4) between tumors and normal bile ducts. Among the four isotypes of GLUTs, GLUT1 and GLUT3 were significantly upregulated in CCA compared with normal tissues (*p* < .001), whereas GLUT2 and GLUT4 were downregulated in CCA (Figure 1(A)). Notably, GLUT2 had a relatively higher basal level of expression in normal cells than GLUT4, and the magnitude of the difference between CCA and normal cells was also larger. The decreased GLUT2 expression then suggested avoiding uptake of unfavorable substrates by cancer cells. In addition, it had been shown previously that GLUT2 had a higher affinity for glucosamine than for glucose [[24]]. Therefore, glucosamine might be harmful to CCA cells and was justified as a basis for the insight study into the underlying mechanisms.

(A) GLUT1 and GLUT3 are significantly upregulated, while GLUT2 and GLUT4 are significantly downregulated in CCA tissues compared with normal counterparts. Notably, basal expression levels of GLUT2 in normal cells are higher than those of GLUT4, and the magnitude of downregulation is larger. (B) Glucosamine significantly reduced CCA cell viability dose-dependently (*p* < .05), while MMNK-1, immortalized nonmalignant cholangiocytes, showed less sensitivity to glucosamine treatment. (C) Half inhibitory concentration (IC50) of glucosamine in different CCA cell lines. MMNK-1, an immortalized nonmalignant cholangiocyte line, has a higher IC50 than CCA cells. The graph represents the average of three biological replications.

### Glucosamine reduced CCA cell viability

The effects of glucosamine on CCA cell viability were investigated using the MTT assay after treatment with various concentrations. Glucosamine significantly reduced the cell viability of CCA in a dose-dependent manner (*p* < .05) (Figure 1(B)). To examine the possible effects on nonmalignant cells, the immortalized cholangiocytes, MMNK-1, were also treated with the same concentrations. The results showed that MMNK-1 was less sensitive to glucosamine, with a half-inhibitory concentration (IC50) significantly higher than that of KKU-213A and slightly higher than that of KKU-055 (Figure 1(C)).

### Glucosamine arrested CCA cells at the G1 phase of the cell cycle

To determine the underlying mechanisms by which glucosamine reduced CCA cell viability, cell cycle analysis using propidium iodide staining and flow cytometry was performed. After treatment with glucosamine, significant cell numbers accumulated at the G1 phase of the cell cycle, dose-dependently (*p* < .05). The subsequent S and G2M phases also showed significantly less accumulation of CCA cells (*p* < .05), corresponding to the G1 arrest (Figure 2(A,B)). The cell cycle regulatory proteins playing roles in the G1 phase, namely, cyclin D1, CDK4, and CDK6, were significantly downregulated in KKU-055 after glucosamine treatment (*p* < .05). In contrast, only CDK6 was significantly reduced in KKU-213A (Figure 2(C,D)). However, the natural CDK inhibitor, p21 protein, was significantly upregulated after glucosamine treatment in both cell lines, suggesting the final outcome of inhibiting cyclin-CDK complex activity.

> **Figure 2.** Glucosamine (GlcN) arrests cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) cell cycle. (A,B) After treatment with glucosamine, the accumulations of CCA cells at the G1 phase were significantly increased, consistent with the reduction of cell distributions in G2/M phases. Histograms are representatives of three independent experiments with the same trend of results, and the bar graphs represent the averages from three biological replications. (C,D) Glucosamine significantly downregulated cyclin dependent kinase (CDK) 6 in both cell lines. Although the expressions of cyclin D1 and CDK4 were discrepant in the two cell lines, p21, a natural cyclin-CDK complex inhibitor, was significantly upregulated, consistent with the final outcome of cell cycle arrest. Western blots are representative of three biological replications with the same trend of results. Graphs show the averaged band intensities of proteins from three biological replications. GAPDH was used as a loading control, and the control groups (0 mM glucosamine) were assigned a factor of 1. **p* < .05, ***p* < .01.

### Glucosamine induced apoptosis of CCA cells

In addition to cell cycle analysis, apoptotic analysis was performed using a double staining method of annexin V and propidium iodide. Glucosamine significantly induced cell apoptosis dose-dependently (*p* < .05), in which its pro-apoptotic effects were more pronounced in KKU-213A (Figure 3(A,B)). Western blots also confirmed significant increases in cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP, the apoptotic markers in both cell lines (*p* < .05) (Figure 3(C,D)). However, the anti-apoptotic XIAP protein was slightly decreased after glucosamine treatment, but the difference was not statistically significant.

Histograms are representatives of three independent experiments with the same trend of results, and the bar graphs represent the averages from three biological replications. (C,D) Pro-apoptotic proteins were significantly increased after glucosamine treatment. However, XIAP, the anti-apoptotic protein was slightly reduced in both cell lines. Western blots are representative of three biological replications with the same trend of results. Graphs show the averaged band intensities of proteins from three biological replications. GAPDH was used as a loading control, and the control groups (0 mM glucosamine) were assigned a factor of 1. **p* < .05, ***p* < .01, p* < .001.

### Glucosamine reduced the molecular weight of EGFR and disrupted STAT3 signaling

Previous studies have shown that glucosamine treatment can inhibit the signaling of the EGFR/STAT3 pathways [[30,31]]. As this oncogenic pathway also plays a vital role in CCA, especially under high-glucose conditions, the effects of glucosamine on EGFR and STAT3 were further investigated. After being treated with glucosamine, the molecular weight of EGFR was reduced as shown by the down-sifting of this protein in the SDS-PAGE (Figure 4(A)). The alteration of EGFR molecular weight also affects the signaling via its downstream as demonstrated by significantly reduced phosphorylation levels of STAT3 at Y705 position in both CCA cell lines (Figure 4(B,C)).

> **Figure 4.** Glucosamine (GlcN) interrupted intracellular signaling and suppressed glycosylation. (A) Glucosamine treatment resulted in a reduced molecular weight of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). This led to (B) suppression of phosphorylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) at Y705. (D) The Western blots demonstrated the molecular weight reduction of glycoprotein 130 (gp130), suggesting glucosamine inhibits the glycosylation of intracellular proteins. (D) Concanavalin A (con A) lectin blots confirmed that glucosamine can inhibit the global *N*-glycosylation of the intracellular proteins in CCA cells. Western blots are representative of three biological replications with the same trend of results. Graphs show the averaged band intensities of proteins from three biological replications. GAPDH was used as a loading control, and the control groups (0 mM glucosamine) were assigned a factor of 1. **p* < .05, ***p* < .01, p* < .001.

### Glucosamine predominantly suppressed high-mannose type N-glycosylation of EGFR

Since the maturity of EGFR requires *N*-glycosylation of the asparagine (N) residues, glucosamine has been reported to suppress *N*-glycosylation in prostate cancer [[30]]. We further hypothesized that glucosamine reduces global *N*-glycosylation of intracellular proteins in CCA cells, resulting in a molecular weight reduction of EGFR and, consequently, disrupting its function. The gp130, a transmembrane glycoprotein with heavy *N*-glycosylation, was used as a representative, and its molecular weight was also downshifted in SDS-PAGE after CCA cells were treated with glucosamine (Figure 4(D)). The effects on suppressing *N*-glycosylation were further confirmed by the lectin blot using concanavalin A (con A), a high-mannose-type *N*-glycosylation binding lectin. As expected, the lectin blots affirmed that glucosamine significantly reduced con A binding glycans in both cell lines (Figure 4(E,F)), suggesting the inhibitory effects on high-mannose type *N*-glycosylation in CCA cells. To further evaluate the inhibitory effects of glucosamine on other types of *N*-glycosylation, WGA, a lectin with a high binding affinity to *N-*acetylglucosamine and sialic acid, and PHA-E, a lectin with high affinity to complex *N*-glycans (bisecting *N*-acetylglucosamine, terminal galactose, and mannose), were used for lectin blots. The lectin blots did not show any significant reduction of WGA binding glycans in CCA cells with glucosamine treatment, and the reduction of PHA-E binding glycans was significantly reduced only in KKU-055 ([Supplementary Figure S1](https://doi.org/10.1080/20565623.2026.2641244)). These results suggested that the effects of glucosamine are primarily inhibitory of high-mannose-type *N*-glycosylation in CCA cells, rather than other glycosylation types.

### High glucose rescued CCA cells from glucosamine treatment

As glucose and glucosamine are transported and phosphorylated for their metabolism through the same pathways, we hypothesized that increasing glucose levels in a culture medium could rescue CCA cells from glucosamine-induced apoptosis. Without glucosamine treatment, increasing the glucose concentration to 25 mM (approximately 2.5 times the regular RPMI) resulted in a significant increase in cell viability in both CCA cell lines. In addition, CCA cells treated with glucosamine showed increased cell viability when 25 mM glucose was supplemented into the media, compared with treatment with glucosamine alone (Figure 5(A)).

> **Figure 5.** High glucose (Glc) rescued glucosamine (GlcN)-treated cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) cells. (A) High glucose promoted CCA cell growth and partially rescued cells treated with glucosamine. (B,C) High glucose also rescued the expression of glycoprotein 130 (gp130) and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). (D,E) *N*-glycosylation in glucosamine-treated CCA cells was significantly increased after high glucose was supplemented, suggesting that increased *N*-glycosylation is a part of the rescuing mechanisms. Western blots and lectin blots are representative of three biological replications with the same trend of results. Graphs show the averaged band intensities of proteins from three biological replications. GAPDH was used as a loading control, and the control groups (0 mM glucosamine without a 25 mM glucose supplement) were assigned a value of 100%. **p* < .05, ***p* < .01, p* < .001.

### High glucose restored high-mannose type N-glycosylation in glucosamine treatment

To investigate whether high glucose can restore *N*-glycosylation in CCA cells along with rescuing cell viability, Western blots of EGFR and gp130, and lectin blots using con A were performed. Without glucosamine, high glucose (25 mM) upregulated gp130 in both cell lines, but upregulated EGFR only in KKU-213A. In the glucosamine treatment, high glucose can restore the expression levels of both EGFR and gp130 in CCA cells, but showed slightly rescuing effects on their molecular weight shifts (Figure 5(B,C)). High-mannose type *N*-glycosylation in CCA cells was significantly restored when glucosamine-treated CCA cells were supplemented with 25 mM glucose, as shown by Con A lectin blots (*p* < .05) (Figure 5(D,E)). In contrast, there was no significant difference in WGA- and PHA-E-binding glycans between glucosamine-treated CCA cells with and without 25 mM glucose supplementation, as shown in WGA and PHA-E lectin blots ([Supplementary Figure S1](https://doi.org/10.1080/20565623.2026.2641244)). These results consistently suggested that the inhibitory effects of glucosamine primarily target high-mannose type glycosylation.

## Discussion

CCA is a cancer with highly deregulated metabolic pathways that are beneficial for its aggression and progression [[4,12]]. High requirements for glucose uptake and the upregulation of genes regulating glycolysis have been revealed, and they serve as potential targets for therapeutic purposes. High glucose consumption provides not only an energy supply but also promotes glycosylation, a post-translational modification process of the proteins that is needed for their appropriate functions [[6,17]]. Several strategies that target these metabolic pathways have become promising methods to improve therapeutic outcomes in cancer patients. Therefore, understanding the insight of metabolic pathways and their networks in cancer cells could help develop precise therapeutic modalities to complement available standard treatments.

In the present study, we found that glucosamine exerted pro-apoptotic effects on CCA cells in vitro by inhibiting high-mannose-type *N*-glycosylation of intracellular proteins and disrupting the EGFR/STAT3 signaling pathways. Since the STAT3 pathway is an upstream regulator of cell cycle regulatory gene transcription, namely cyclin D1 and its associated cyclin dependent kinases 4 and 6, the inhibition of STAT3 activation, hence, resulted in CCA cell cycle arrest. Moreover, STAT3 directly activates transcription of the anti-apoptotic XIAP gene; disrupting STAT3 signaling can lead to significant apoptosis in CCA cells. The present findings align with reports on prostate cancer, indicating that glucosamine inhibits global *N*-glycosylation in cells and blocks the binding of interleukin-6 (IL-6) to its receptor [[30,31]]. The reduced high-mannose-type *N*-glycosylation also affected EGFR stability, thereby reducing its expression levels, which in turn impaired STAT3 activation. Since the EGFR/STAT3 and IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 pathways are also implicated in CCA cells [[18,32]], our findings support the mechanisms underlying the effects of glucosamine in prostate cancers. Moreover, glucosamine also suppressed the phosphorylation of other EGFR downstream targets, such as ERK and AKT, in non-small cell lung cancer [[33]] and hepatocellular carcinoma [[25]]. These suggest that glucosamine may have a wide range of effects, including its impact on the post-translational modification of essential proteins in cancer cells.

Our study also demonstrated that high glucose levels can increase *N*-glycosylation, as shown by lectin blots with Con A, WGA, and PHA-E. However, the rescued *N*-glycosylation was predominantly detected by Con A, suggesting that glucosamine exerts a major inhibitory effect on high-mannose-type *N*-glycosylation. CCA cell viability was partially rescued from glucosamine treatment when glucose concentration was increased. These findings might result from metabolic competition between glucose and glucosamine in energy metabolism, as reported in lung adenocarcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma cells [[24]]. Additionally, our previous studies have shown that high glucose can upregulate EGFR and FOXM1 [[34]], which are upstream of the affected signaling molecules, such as STAT3, at the transcriptional level. Therefore, the rescuing effects of high glucose on glucosamine-treated cells might be associated with multiple mechanisms.

The present study also demonstrated differential expression of major GLUTs in CCA compared with their normal counterparts, suggesting dynamic regulation of energy metabolism and other functional roles of glucose and related sugars. GLUT1 and GLUT3, which have a high affinity to glucose, were significantly upregulated in CCA, consistent with the high glucose requirement of CCA cells as reported in our previous studies [[12,32,34]]. In contrast, the expression of GLUT2, a major GLUT used in cellular glucosamine uptake with lower affinity to glucose, was significantly downregulated in CCA tissues compared with normal bile ducts. This suggested that CCA cells undergo reprogramming of glucosamine uptake and metabolism, potentially avoiding the toxicity caused by excessive intracellular glucosamine. While the downregulation of GLUT2 could affect the transport of glucosamine into CCA cells, glucosamine can also be imported via GLUT1 and GLUT3. One study has shown that the affinity of GLUT1 for glucose and glucosamine is similar, as indicated by their Km values [[35]]. Thus, the upregulation of GLUT1 in CCA cells might compensate for the decreased GLUT2, and glucosamine could reach toxic intracellular levels in CCA cells. A study on chondrocytes also found that glucosamine can disrupt glucose transport and might thereby synergize with the anti-tumor effects in our model [[36]]. On the other hand, downregulation of GLUT2 in CCA cells could hamper the therapeutic potential of glucosamine, and the delivery of glucosamine to cancer cells warrants further investigation. Moreover, the higher affinity of GLUT2 for glucosamine could be overcome by elevated glucose levels, thereby compromising glucosamine’s effects in patients with hyperglycemia [[24]]. The findings in the present study, then, not only provide insight into the mechanisms of how glucosamine affects CCA cells but also support previous reports that controlling plasma glucose could benefit the treatment of CCA in patients with DM [[32]]. Nevertheless, the toxicity of glucosamine at the in vitro effective dose could be a concern for clinical translation. A current recommendation for an oral supplementary dose of glucosamine is 1500 mg daily [[20]], which is approximately half the effective dose from the in vitro experiment. However, the median lethal dose (LD50) of glucosamine in animals is particular high and has been reported up to 8000 mg/kg, which is higher than the IC50 reported in the present study [[37]]. Therefore, further studies are needed to clarify the benefits and risks of repurposing glucosamine for cancer treatment.

Epidemiological studies have reported that glucosamine intake is associated with a reduced risk of certain cancers, including lung malignancies [[21,23]], colorectal cancer [[21,35]], and kidney cancer [[21]]. On the other hand, the controversies remain on the cancer type-specific effects of glucosamine. It has been suggested that glucosamine supplements could have both null effects on cancer development [[38]] and be associated with an increased risk of skin, prostate, and overall cancers [[21]]. However, the effects of glucosamine on CCA had not been previously investigated. Therefore, our study is the first to specifically report the effects of glucosamine on the progression of CCA.

Our study, nevertheless, has some limitations that should be addressed in future studies. First, the CCA cell lines used are from patients with liver fluke-associated CCA and from patients in a region with a high prevalence of diabetes [[39]], which might affect the basal glucose metabolism of the cells as a result of hyperglycemic memory [[40]]. Additional studies with non-liver fluke-associated CCA cell lines would help clarify the effects of glucosamine on CCA in general. Second, although glucosamine reduced high-mannose type *N*-glycosylation levels in many proteins, the novel candidates for therapeutic targets that are affected have yet to be identified. Finally, the therapeutic effects of glucosamine on CCA progression should be confirmed in vivo, so that the pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and safety profile of glucosamine could be better understood for clinical translation.

## Conclusion

In summary, glucosamine was shown to exert anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects against CCA cells in vitro by suppressing intracellular high-mannose type *N*-glycosylation and interfering with EGFR/STAT3 signaling. Since glucosamine is currently used as a food supplement and alternative treatment for some diseases, it should be further investigated in vivo for future clinical translation and potentially repurposing for CCA treatment.
